These words aren't intensifiers

2026-11-21
:: tags:  #post  #rant

This is a small list of useful words or phrases I am seeing misused as general purpose intensifiers. For the most part, these are lost causes. Linguists say language is always changing, and I think it's pointless to try and take up arms against that, even when someone is using words wrong.

tldr: Exponentially. Ontologically. Epistemic. Cognitive dissonance. Bad faith. I love most of these words and phrases, but they're hard to use without taking on the pallor of using a commonly-misused-word, even if correctly. These phrases are suffering the same loss-of-utility that the word "literally" has suffered for centuries.

btw - I'm a person writing this by hand. I use bolds and I add links as appropriate. I will never put LLM-generated prose on your screen, even if it looks like that because I use markdown.

Literally is a classic example of a word which would have a useful meaning if it were not so widely used as an intensifier. I'm not here to fight a war lost centuries ago, though. But the idea of a "literal meaning" of a word is a concept too useful to do away with, and one doesn't want to resort to phrases like "literally literally".

I'm instead proposing, as alternatives, phrases like non-figuratively, non-hyperbolically, and in the literal sense.

For example,

Then the doctor gave me a hard pill to swallow. Non-figuratively, it was huge. Turns out it's a suppository.

or

Resistors are -- and I'm not being hyperbolic here -- a dime a dozen.

These have the added benefit of being more specific than "literal". But I think one can just replace "literally" with "in a literal sense". I could be wrong, butwhen one chooses their words in this way, it seems to be uninterpretable as intensifier.

Break a leg. In a literal sense. (I do not like you) (I hope your performance fails so spectacularly as to render you immobile).

I start with "literally" since you can just use it for the rest of these. I will provide a link to BlueSky for each of these terms, to provide examples. Here is how people are using "literally" online. As of this posting: Lots of literal use, lots of figurative use, and some bickering over the meaning of the word.

Exponentially usefully describes a relationship between two quantifiable things. It can be used to describe four basic shapes: Exponential growth, exponential decay, but also exponential sigmoids, either increasing from 0 to saturation, or decreasing from saturation to 0.

As an aside: Most exponential relationships we see in real life are sigmoidal! A house fire exponentially grows, until it starts to run out of fuel. A virus spreads exponentially, until it runs out of uninfected hosts. This kind of spread is modeled with exponential growth, until it takes the shape of an (upside down) exponential decay. That's a sigmoid, baby.

Relationships between qualitative ideas can make sense under the word "exponential" too. Perhaps "our love grew exponentially with each day". Even if an exaggeration, this implies a metric over a qualitative value (love) with respect to time.

Using the word exponential to describe relationships between two qualitative ideas might also imply a third shared variable (such as time). Sometimes, I feel that I need an exponentially better quality coffee bean to get a linear increase in the perceived quality of a cup of coffee. These, too, are sensible statements. I've got no qualms with the word "exponential" being used with such poetic license.

But people will say things like "Tea is exponentially better than coffee", which is a total nonsense statement. There is no implicit spectrum on which tea and coffee both exist.

The word "exponential" is useful (and, for belonging to math, perhaps one of the few places where the prescriptivist mindset is always right). But if you want to clarify you are using the word exponential in its literal meaning, you might need to instead say "exponentially related" or "non-figuratively exponential". You might even say "super-linear" to be more general.

Here is how people are using "exponentially" online: "Just got exponentially better" (someones Saturday, upon seeing a pair of breasts, not how it works) or "Dogs are smarter than Trump. Exponentially smarter" (not how it works).

Ontologically is perhaps one of the funnier general-purpose intensifiers which have grown to common use nowadays, especially with the phrase "ontologically evil".

In the most generous interpretation, I think people often mean "fundamentally" or "inherently" when they use the word "ontologically". This word is tied with "epistemically" for being the most embarrassingly-misused word here. When did people even start misusing this word? Where did this come from? Google Trends shows its use rising since May 2022 and spiking in May 2026.

Even if you're using the word "ontologically" correctly, it will be a small bucket in an ocean of people misusing the word to chase the appearance of well-founded intellectualism. You must contend with the possibility that your intended audience might have adopted the useful heuristic to dismiss anyone who uses the word "ontological".

Lucky for me, I never find myself in the context to use the word "ontological". I don't have an alternative to provide. Here is how people are using "ontologically" online: mostly in the phrase "ontologically evil".

But in the past, I would use the word epistemic frequently. It's very useful to be able to talk about thought and knowledge and the inherent uncertainty behind it. I was a scientist doing research about mechanistically acquiring knowledge, after all. Unfortunately for me, I think "epistemically" or "epistemic" are words simply best avoided, like "ontologically".

I think we can trace the rise of the word "epistemic" or "epistemically" into common parlance with the divided reality we increasingly live in. This has been discussed extensively and is not worth getting into here. Google Trends shows its use rising since November 2025 and spiking in February 2026.

This one isn't so bad, though. Here is how people are using "epistemic" online: largely correctly, with many of the misuses in jest, such as "epistemic gooning".

For my writing, I would tend to avoid this when it's not necessary. But I'm just a no-name blogger on the internet. Don't place too much weight into what I write.

There's no conclusion to this post. I'm shaking my fists at a cloud, complaining about words being misused, waving up my arms and saying "well, what can ya do". But hey, while you're here, I've got more shaking-my-fist-at-cloud to do:

  • Cognitive dissonance is the internal feeling one has while holding conflicting beliefs. I guess enough people have misunderstood the idea that now, one says "the cognitive dissonance" in reaction to someone exhibiting hypocrisy. Here is how people are using the phrase "cognitive dissonance" online. This is maybe the worst of these.
  • "Bad faith" was a useful phrase to point out that someone is not sincerely arguing from a position they hold. Now, I think the meta is to be the first to accuse your interlocutor of bad faith, since the internet is an elementary school playground. By being the first to accuse, a retort that "actually, you are the one acting in bad faith" sounds like "I know you are but what am I"? This is very frustrating to me.
